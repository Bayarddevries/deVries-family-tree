# The World of the deVries/Spence/Setter Family
## Community & Historical Context Findings — Red River Métis of Manitoba

*Compiled for the deVries family tree. Scope: community/historical context only (individual genealogical records are handled by a separate agent). Dates: focused 1790s–1900.*

**How to read this file:** every claim is tagged **[VERIFIED]** (read directly in the cited source) or **[INFERENCE]** (a reasoned connection drawn from the cited sources, not directly stated). Every source is cited with author/title/publisher/URL. Several primary sources were read in full this session (Ens 1996 excerpts; redriverancestry family pages; Manitoba Historical Society pages; Barkwell's Legislative Assembly of Manitoba Métis Members; the Metis Museum buffalo-hunt material).

## RANK-ORDERED: Most interesting findings for a reader

1. **[VERIFIED] The family was directly embedded in the making of Manitoba.** David Spence (1824–1885) was a delegate for St. Anne's/Poplar Point at the Convention of Forty (Jan 25–Feb 10, 1870) that produced the provisional government with Louis Riel as President, and he became the first MLA from Poplar Point (1870–74). His nephew John Norquay — raised by David's parents — became the first Premier of Manitoba. This is a family whose members sat at the table of the 1869–70 Resistance.
2. **[VERIFIED] The family crossed the entire arc of the fur-trade-to-farming transition.** James Spence Sr (c.1753–1795, HBC) and Andrew Setter (1777–1870, Orkney HBC voyageur) were fur-trade-era founders; by the 1870s–80s their descendants (George Setter, David Spence) were river-lot farmers at Poplar Point/High Bluff claiming Métis scrip. Four generations span the homeland-to-hinterland shift Ens describes.
3. **[VERIFIED] The Buffalo hunt and hivernant world was the economic engine of White Horse Plain / St. François Xavier.** Cuthbert Grant founded Grantown (later St. François Xavier) in 1824 partly to keep the Métis buffalo economy inside British territory; the parish's captain-of-the-hunt tradition was a model of Métis self-government. John Norquay and his wife Elizabeth Setter farmed at High Bluff and "did some fur trading at White Horse Plain."
4. **[VERIFIED] The country-marriage ("à la façon du pays") system produced this family.** Andrew Setter wed Peggy Spence (daughter of James Spence Sr) around 1810 by country custom; Rev. John West baptized and married them formally in 1821. James Spence Jr's first wife Mary was a Stone (Assiniboine) woman; his second, Jane Morwick, was the daughter of an Indigenous woman and an Orkney father.
5. **[VERIFIED] River lots, not square-mile sections, shaped these communities.** The Assiniboine valley from Winnipeg to Portage was settled on river lots before Confederation; St. Anne's Poplar Point, St. Margaret's High Bluff and St. Mary's la Prairie (Portage) churches anchored each settlement.
6. **[INFERENCE] Everyday Métis life here was a seasonal rhythm:** sow in spring, summer buffalo hunt (dried meat/pemmican), harvest, fall hunt, and often winter hivernant camps on the plains — all within a mixed economy of small-scale farming, cart freighting and robe production. This is the documented pattern for the parishes in which the family lived.

## 1. THE COMMUNITIES

### St. François Xavier / White Horse Plain (Grantown)
- **[VERIFIED]** After the 1821 HBC–NWC merger and the fixing of the 49th parallel (placing Pembina's Métis settlement in the US), HBC Governor George Simpson saw Cuthbert Grant as a leader who could hold the Métis to British soil and keep them "at a safe distance" from the colony. Grant brought ~50 families from Pembina to White Horse Plain in 1824, founding Grantown. The HBC made him "Warden of the Plains" (salary £200/yr) and he furnished a mill and other facilities. Source: Irene Ternier Gordon, "Blog #36 White Horse Plain / Cuthbert Grant / St. François Xavier," Norwest Historian, 14 Nov 2024, https://norwesthistorian.wordpress.com/2024/11/14/blog-36-white-horse-plain-cuthbert-grant-st-francois-xavier/ ; Dictionary of Canadian Biography, "GRANT, CUTHBERT (d.1854)," http://www.biographi.ca/en/bio/grant_cuthbert_1854_8E.html
- **[VERIFIED]** The area was known as "White Horse Plain" from the days of the buffalo hunt (a legend of a ghostly white horse is commemorated by a landmark statue on Hwy 26). Grantown was renamed St. François Xavier after Grant's death in 1854. Source: Gordon, Norwest Historian; Rosemary Malaher, "Historical Tour: Highway 26," *Manitoba History* 8 (Autumn 1984), http://www.mhs.mb.ca/docs/mb_history/08/highway26.shtml
- **[VERIFIED]** St. François Xavier began as a mission of St. Boniface, became a parish in 1833 (the second Roman Catholic parish in Manitoba); Grey Nuns opened a convent school there in 1850 (ran to 1970). The community was the largest centre of Red River cart manufacture. Grant was long elected "Captain of the Hunt" of the Métis buffalo hunts. Source: Malaher, *Manitoba History* 8.
- **[VERIFIED]** The buffalo economy defined White Horse Plain: its brigade(s) headed directly west along the Assiniboine (bypassing Pembina) from the late 1830s, re-orienting the parish toward a plains life; Trottier and other brigades began wintering ever farther west by the 1850s. Source: M. F. S. (Brenda) Macdougall et al., "Rooted in Mobility: Metis Buffalo Hunting Brigades," *Manitoba History* 71 (2015), https://www.mhs.mb.ca/docs/mb_history/71/metisbrigades.shtml
- **[FAMILY CONNECTION / VERIFIED]** John Norquay and his wife **Elizabeth Setter** (daughter of George Setter) "farmed at High Bluff, did some fur trading at White Horse Plain and farmed again." Source: redriverancestry.ca, "Premier John Norquay," https://www.redriverancestry.ca/NORQUAY-JOHN-1841.php

### Poplar Point & High Bluff (David Spence's home & MLA constituency)
- **[VERIFIED]** The modern Hwy 26 (Assiniboine Valley) runs through St. François Xavier, Baie St. Paul, Poplar Point and High Bluff to Portage; all were settled on **river lots** before Confederation (1870). The farms along the river "became popular and river lots began to fill up between Headingly and Portage." Source: Malaher, *Manitoba History* 8.
- **[VERIFIED]** St. Anne's, Poplar Point, was an **Anglican** church; the old log church (Red River frame construction) still stands, completed in 1864 after three winters of work by the settlers, with a bell brought from York Factory. St. Margaret's, High Bluff, was also built by local people. Source: Malaher, *Manitoba History* 8.
- **[VERIFIED]** David Spence represented "St Anne's" — the church parish at Poplar Point — at the Convention of Forty; from 1870–74 he was the **first representative from Poplar Point in the Legislative Assembly of Manitoba**, and was also Justice of the Peace for Poplar Point and Marquette. Source: redriverancestry.ca, "David Spence (1824–1885)," https://www.redriverancestry.ca/SPENCE-DAVID-1834.php ; Manitoba Historical Society, "Memorable Manitobans: Convention of Forty," http://www.mhs.mb.ca/docs/people/conventionofforty.shtml
- **[VERIFIED]** In 1870 David Spence's family was on **River Lot 62, Poplar Point**; in 1877 they moved to lot #62 at Poplar Point. Source: redriverancestry.ca "David Spence"; Lawrence J. Barkwell, "Legislative Assembly of Manitoba Métis Members," Louis Riel Institute, https://www.metismuseum.ca/media/document.php/15065 (full text cached locally).
- **[VERIFIED]** George Setter (1815–1899) received several Western land grants in the High Bluff–Poplar Point area (Burnside–Marquette districts) in 1879, 1880 and 1883; he claimed scrip for his children in High Bluff in 1875; he and his widow Jessie lived out their lives at High Bluff/Poplar Point. His son Roderick McKenzie Setter (b.1856) farmed in the Poplar Point–High Bluff area. Source: redriverancestry.ca, "George Setter (1815–1899)," https://www.redriverancestry.ca/SETTER-GEORGE-1815.php

### Portage la Prairie / St. Mary's la Prairie
- **[VERIFIED]** In the early 1850s Rev. William Cockran (Church Missionary Society) established a settlement and church community at Portage la Prairie named **St. Mary's**. East of Portage, the local people themselves built the Anglican churches of St. Margaret's (High Bluff) and St. Anne's (Poplar Point). Source: Malaher, *Manitoba History* 8.
- **[VERIFIED]** St. Mary's la Prairie (Portage) was one of the English-speaking Métis parishes of the region; Kenneth McKenzie represented "St. Mary's" at the Convention of Forty. Source: MHS, "Convention of Forty."
- **[FAMILY CONNECTION / VERIFIED]** Several Spence/Setter descendants married at Portage la Prairie (e.g., David Spence's daughter Mary Ann married Ernest Riggs there in 1887; son William married Martha McNab in 1909). Jane (Morwick) Spence died at Poplar Point NW of Portage in 1875. Source: redriverancestry.ca "David Spence."

## 2. THE BUFFALO HUNT & HIVERNANT LIFE

### Organization of the organized hunts
- **[VERIFIED]** By the 1830s every major Red River Métis buffalo hunt had a common form and governance: three main parties (Pembina, St. Boniface, and St. François Xavier) gathered at Pembina for the summer hunt; captains (per-parish) retained authority in the settlement but chose a "governor of the hunt" for the trip who regulated movement and refereed disputes. Source: Wikipedia, "Métis buffalo hunting" (citing Gaudry, Macdougall, Ross), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%A9tis_buffalo_hunting ; The Metis Heritage Project, "Our History," https://www.themetishp.org/social-media/
- **[VERIFIED]** The 1840 summer hunt, as described by Alexander Ross: 1,210 Red River carts, 620 hunters, 650 women, 360 boys and girls, 403 buffalo runners, 655 cart horses, 586 oxen, 542 dogs — 1,630 souls. The carts formed a defensive circle ("circumvallation") with tents inside; a general council at Pembina chose the leaders and set the laws of the hunt "all without the aid of writing materials." It returned with ~1,089,000 lb of buffalo meat (dried meat of ~10,000 buffalo). Source: Macdougall et al., "Rooted in Mobility," *Manitoba History* 71; Wikipedia "Métis buffalo hunting."
- **[VERIFIED]** Men hunted; **women maintained the camps and preserved the meat** — an enormous and time-critical labour. All family members participated. Source: Wikipedia "Métis buffalo hunting"; Macdougall et al. *Manitoba History* 71.
- **[VERIFIED]** Hunt scale grew: 540 carts (1820), 689 (1825), 820 (1830), 970 (1835), 1,210 (1840). Source: Wikipedia "Métis buffalo hunting."

### Hivernant (wintering) way of life
- **[VERIFIED]** Métis "hivernants"/winterers left the settlements each fall to live in small temporary communities on the plains (Turtle Mountain, Souris Valley, Qu'Appelle, Wood Mountain, Buffalo Lake) to hunt buffalo for the robe trade and to trap. Sites ranged from a few families to camps of ~1,000 (e.g., Buffalo Lake in the 1870s). Cabins were built in late fall and usually abandoned after one or two winters. Source: Ens, *Homeland to Hinterland* (excerpts read this session); Wikipedia "Métis buffalo hunting."
- **[VERIFIED]** Hivernants returned to Fort Garry in May–June to trade furs, robes, meat and leather, preferring to arrive by the Queen's birthday (May 24), a day of horse racing at the fort. Source: Ens, *Homeland to Hinterland*.
- **[VERIFIED]** From the 1840s to 1870s the buffalo-robe trade increasingly drew whole Métis families away from Red River for the fall/winter and sometimes years; one "could not winter in the Northwest and practise subsistence agriculture on the river lots." The trade forced a choice between subsistence farming in Red River or a hivernant existence on the plains. Source: Ens, *Homeland to Hinterland*.

### Ens's core argument (Homeland to Hinterland, 1996)
- **[VERIFIED]** Gerhard Ens, *Homeland to Hinterland: The Changing Worlds of the Red River Métis in the Nineteenth Century*, U Toronto Press, 1996 (ISBN 0-8020-7822-2 paper). Thesis: Métis identity was not defined by biology/blood but by the **economic and social niche** they carved in the fur trade; the Métis adapted quickly to changed economic conditions after the 1840s rather than being overwhelmed. The book's case studies are the parishes of **St. François Xavier and St. Andrew's**. Source: Ens, *Homeland to Hinterland* (intro and ch. 4 read this session), https://dokumen.pub/homeland-to-hinterland-the-changing-worlds-of-the-red-river-metis-in-the-nineteenth-century-9781442675827.html
- **[VERIFIED]** Ens frames the era with Irene Spry's "great transformation": resources moved from common-property, to open-access, to private-property regimes (1840s–1890s). He applies the concept of **proto-industrialization**: Métis families combined subsistence farming/hunting with market-oriented production (especially buffalo robes and leather goods) — a "cottage industry." Source: Ens, *Homeland to Hinterland*, intro.
- **[VERIFIED]** The buffalo **robe** was the main Métis fur-trade item after 1840. Robes (skins tanned with hair on) fetched up to $10 each by the 1870s; women tanned them (4 days' work per robe). HBC shipped 10–12,000 robes/yr by the 1840s and 18,000 in 1865. St Paul cart arrivals rose from 6 (1844) to 1,400+ (1865); St Paul fur-trade value from $1,400 (1844) to $300,000 (1865). Source: Ens, *Homeland to Hinterland*, ch. 4.
- **[VERIFIED]** Crop failures of 1844–48 (only 1845 produced a sufficiency; famine by Aug 1848) pushed many Métis away from agriculture and into the expanding fur/robe trade. Source: Ens, *Homeland to Hinterland*, ch. 4.
- **[INFERENCE for THIS family]** The White Horse Plain / Portage-area Métis — the population group to which the Spence/Setter families belonged — were precisely Ens's St. François Xavier case study: a buffalo-hunt-and-robe-trade economy transitioning to river-lot farming. When the buffalo herds collapsed (largely gone by the late 1870s; the last great herds by 1879) and scrip/land grants were issued in the 1870s–80s, families like the Spences and Setters settled into permanent farming — exactly the trajectory documented for George Setter's land grants (1879–83) and David Spence's River Lot 62 farm.

## 3. THE 1869–70 RED RIVER RESISTANCE & THE CONVENTION OF FORTY

- **[VERIFIED]** In 1869 Canada acquired Rupert's Land from the HBC and began surveying Red River; on Oct 11, 1869, Louis Riel stopped the surveyors. Métis formed the Métis National Committee; resistance culminated in the Manitoba Act (12 May 1870) creating the Province of Manitoba. Source: Wikipedia "Métis buffalo hunting"; redriverancestry.ca "David Spence."
- **[VERIFIED]** The **Convention of Forty** met 25 Jan–10 Feb 1870: 20 French and 20 English representatives drew up a list of rights for Ottawa. It then proclaimed itself a provisional government with Riel as President, and sent Father Ritchot, Alfred Scott and Judge John Black to Ottawa. Riel acted as English interpreter for French delegates; James Ross as French interpreter for English delegates. Source: MHS, "Convention of Forty" (after L. J. Barkwell, *Manitoba's Provisional Government of 1870: The Convention of Forty*, Gabriel Dumont Institute, 2011).
- **[VERIFIED]** **David Spence (1824–1885) was the delegate for Ste. Anne's (Poplar Point)** on the English side of the Convention of Forty. He was elected to the first Manitoba legislature (1870–74) as the first MLA for Poplar Point, and was a Justice of the Peace. Source: MHS "Convention of Forty"; Barkwell, "Legislative Assembly of Manitoba Métis Members"; redriverancestry.ca "David Spence."
- **[VERIFIED]** Barkwell's official biography: "David married Catherine Hallett on February 15, 1844 at St. John's. She was the daughter of Henry Hallett and Catherine Parenteau. David represented the parish of St. Anne's at the Convention of Forty. In 1877 the family moved to lot #62 at Poplar Point. He was elected as the first MLA from Poplar Point. He was also a Justice of the Peace for Poplar Point and Marquette. Métis scrip was issued to David and Catherine on October 2 [1875/1876]." Source: Barkwell, "Legislative Assembly of Manitoba Métis Members," Louis Riel Institute.
- **[VERIFIED]** The Resistance was not monolithic along ethnic lines. The English-speaking "Portage group" (e.g., Frederick Bird of Portage la Prairie) **opposed** Riel; Bird was imprisoned by Riel at one point. Meanwhile David Spence — an English-speaking Poplar Point delegate — participated in the Convention. The English half of the convention included many St. Andrew's/English-Métis figures (John Black, Donald Gunn, Alfred Boyd). Source: Barkwell, "Legislative Assembly of Manitoba Métis Members"; MHS "Convention of Forty."
- **[VERIFIED]** Manitoba's first provincial election was held 27 Dec 1870; John Norquay was elected for the **High Bluff** district and later became the first Premier of Manitoba (1878–87). Source: redriverancestry.ca "George Setter"; "Premier John Norquay."
- **[VERIFIED]** John Norquay (1841–1889) was the **grandson of Jane Morwick** — David Spence's mother (Jane's first husband Oman Norquay died on White Horse Plain in 1820; she then married James Spence Jr in 1822; John Norquay was raised by his Spence grandparents after his parents' deaths). John married **Elizabeth Setter** (daughter of George Setter) at Portage la Prairie in 1862. So Premier Norquay links BOTH the Spence and Setter lines of this family. Source: redriverancestry.ca "Premier John Norquay"; "James Spence (1778–1857)".
- **[VERIFIED]** Post-1870, Métis land rights were extinguished through the **Half-breed (Métis) scrip** system — the source records that anchor this family's genealogy. David & Catherine Spence, John & Elizabeth (Setter) Norquay, and George Setter (for his children) all claimed scrip in the 1870s. Source: Barkwell "Legislative Assembly"; redriverancestry.ca family pages; MNO Métis Historic Timeline, https://www.metisnation.org/about-the-mno/metis-timeline/
- **[VERIFIED]** The Métis dispersal after 1870 (Ens's "homeland to hinterland" ch. 7) — many Red River Métis left Manitoba westward — but families like the Spences and Setters, who took scrip and stayed on their river lots, became rooted prairie farmers. Source: Ens, *Homeland to Hinterland* (ch. 7); redriverancestry.ca land-grant records.

## 4. THE FUR TRADE WORLD & COUNTRY MARRIAGE

### The HBC / York Factory / Orkney voyageur connection
- **[VERIFIED]** The HBC recruited heavily from the **Orkney Islands** (Scotland), including boys of 12–14, because wars (1778–83, 1793–1815) stripped the Orkneys of seamen and the Royal Navy imposed a height restriction; the HBC agent could only meet quota with short, young Orkneymen. Source: redriverancestry.ca "James Spence (1778–1857)."
- **[VERIFIED]** **James Spence Sr (c.1753–1795)** was an HBC man whose daughter Peggy (Margaret) married Andrew Setter; his will names "Nestichio, daughter of the deceased Isaac Batt," confirming an Indigenous first wife / country marriage pattern in the founder generation. Source: redriverancestry.ca "Andrew Setter (1777–1870)" (citing James Spence 1795 will; cross-checked via DCB/Dictionary of Canadian Biography per the metis-genealogy-research skill).
- **[VERIFIED]** **James Spence Jr (1778–1857)**, born Orphir, Orkney, hired by HBC in 1797 as a **Tailor**; sailed Stromness→York Factory aboard the *King George*; worked inland (Saskatchewan District) until going "to the Colony" (Red River) in 1818. As HBC tailor for Red River he made outfits for Rev. John West's St. John's Boy's School. Source: redriverancestry.ca "James Spence (1778–1857)."
- **[VERIFIED]** **Andrew Setter (1777–1870)**, born Westray, Orkney, HBC Labourer at York Factory from 1800; voyaged inland with Chief Factor William Tomison; stationed at Fort Vermilion/Paint River and Fort Edmonton (1816–18); from 1819–23 a steersman/voyageur in the Upper Red River District; retired to Red River (St. Andrews) in 1823; died at Poplar Point in 1870. Source: redriverancestry.ca "Andrew Setter (1777–1870)."

### Country marriage ("à la façon du pays")
- **[VERIFIED]** The country-marriage system (formal marriage by Indigenous/country custom, later solemnized by clergy) produced the Métis families of the fur trade. Andrew Setter began a "connubial relationship" with **Peggy Spence** around 1810; on Rev. John West's first trip west in Jan 1821 he **baptized and married Andrew and Margaret and baptized their children**. Source: redriverancestry.ca "Andrew Setter."
- **[VERIFIED]** James Spence Jr's first wife was **Mary (a Stone/Assiniboine Indian)**; after her death (1821) he married widow **Jane (née Morwick) Norquay** (daughter of an Indigenous woman and James Morwick of Orkney) in 1822, and his and Jane's children were baptized the same day. Source: redriverancestry.ca "James Spence (1778–1857)."
- **[VERIFIED]** The 1821 HBC–NWC merger threw ~1,300 fur-trade employees out of work and turned many retired voyageurs and their families into Red River settlers — the mechanism by which Orkney voyageurs like Spence and Setter became the patriarchs of Red River Métis families. Source: redriverancestry.ca "James Spence"; "Andrew Setter"; "James Spence (1778–1857)."
- **[INFERENCE for THIS family]** The Cree/Assiniboine (Stone) and other Indigenous roots on both the Spence and Morwick lines, joined to Orkney Scots (Spence, Setter, Kennedy, Morwick, Halcrow), place this family squarely in the classic fur-trade country-marriage pattern that Ens, Giraud and others describe as the origin of the Métis Nation. The family's own marriages of that pattern (country union then formal baptism/marriage, e.g., Andrew & Peggy Setter 1821) are directly documented.

## 5. EVERYDAY LIFE: ECONOMY, RELIGION, EDUCATION

### Economy & the hunting-to-farming transition
- **[VERIFIED]** The Métis of the Assiniboine parishes ran a **mixed economy**: subsistence farming on river lots + seasonal buffalo hunts (summer dried-meat/pemmican hunt, fall hunt) + cart freighting + buffalo-robe/leather production for market. Pemmican and dried meat provisioned the fur-trade brigades and were sold to the HBC. Source: Wikipedia "Métis buffalo hunting"; Ens, *Homeland to Hinterland*; Macdougall et al., *Manitoba History* 71.
- **[VERIFIED]** The pemmican trade was so central that the HBC depended on it into the 1870s; the 1849 **Sayer Trial** (freeing Métis to trade freely) ended the HBC monopoly and opened a new economic era — Metis "commerce est libre." Source: Ens, *Homeland to Hinterland*; Wikipedia "Métis buffalo hunting."
- **[VERIFIED]** By the 1849 census some Métis had abandoned farms for the plains; area under cultivation in Red River rose from 3,504 acres (1835) to 6,392 (1849), but unevenly. After the buffalo collapse (late 1870s) and scrip/land grants (1870s–80s), the population settled into farming. Source: Ens, *Homeland to Hinterland*.
- **[FAMILY CONNECTION / VERIFIED]** Andrew Setter's 1827 Red River census entry shows the everyday farm: house, barn, stable; 4 horses, 8 cows, 2 oxen; 2 carts, a plough and harrow, a canoe; 5 acres under cultivation. James Spence Jr's 1827 entry (River Lot 247, Fort Rouge): house and stable; a horse and mare, 4 cows, 2 calves, an ox, one swine; plough, harrow, cart, canoe; 7 acres under cultivation. Source: redriverancestry.ca "Andrew Setter"; "James Spence (1778–1857)."
- **[INFERENCE for THIS family]** David Spence's River Lot 62 farm at Poplar Point, George Setter's High Bluff land grants, and John Norquay's High Bluff farming are all instances of the final stage of this transition: permanent, mixed, river-lot agriculture in the Assiniboine valley after 1870 — the "hinterland" endpoint of Ens's arc.

### Religion (Church of England / St. John's) & education
- **[VERIFIED]** The family was Anglican/Church of England. David Spence and Catherine Hallett married at **St. John's** (1844); James Spence Jr is buried at St. John's; St. Anne's Poplar Point and St. Margaret's High Bluff were the local Anglican churches. Source: redriverancestry.ca family pages; Malaher, *Manitoba History* 8.
- **[VERIFIED]** Rev. John West (CMS/HBC chaplain, arrived 1821) built the first Red River church and **St. John's Boy's School** near the Forks, recruiting Indigenous youngsters; James Spence (the tailor) made the boys' school outfits. Source: redriverancestry.ca "James Spence (1778–1857)."
- **[VERIFIED]** Education in the French-Catholic St. François Xavier parish was run by the Grey Nuns (convent school from 1850). Source: Malaher, *Manitoba History* 8.
- **[INFERENCE for THIS family]** English-speaking Anglican Métis (Spence, Setter, Norquay) sat in the orbit of St. John's, Kildonan and the St. Andrews/St. Mary's missionary network — the CMS Protestant wing of the Red River Settlement that Ens and Pannekoek identify as socially distinct from the Catholic St. François Xavier community, even though the family's buffalo-trade roots (White Horse Plain) were shared.

### Everyday rhythm (synthesis)
- **[INFERENCE]** A plausible composite of a Spence/Setter year: spring sowing → summer buffalo hunt (with women processing meat) → haying/harvest → fall hunt → some families wintered on the plains as hivernants (or stayed to farm) → return/trade at Fort Garry by late May. Daily life combined the seasonal hunt with river-lot farming, cart-building/repair, horse-keeping, and for many, participation in the free trade to St. Paul. This is a documented pattern for the parishes in which this family lived (Ens, Macdougall, Malaher, Metis Heritage Project).
